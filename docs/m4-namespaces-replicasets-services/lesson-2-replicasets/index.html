ReplicaSets, labels and selectors

▶ M4 · ReplicaSets, labels & selectorsOpen fullscreen ↗

In M3 you ran the frontend as a single Pod. You placed it by hand. If it had died, it would have stayed dead until you noticed and applied it again. One Pod is a single point of failure, and a hard ceiling on load. Those are the two problems a distributed system exists to avoid. This part introduces the object that removes both: the ReplicaSet. And underneath it, the mechanism the whole rest of Kubernetes leans on: labels and selectors. Give this part real attention. Labels look simple, and it is tempting to skim them. But from here to the end of the course, one question keeps coming back: "how does this object find the Pods it manages?" It always has the same answer.

Why one Pod is never enough​

Two forces make a single Pod a non-starter for anything real.

  • Availability. If the Pod's node reboots, or the container crashes in a way a restart cannot fix, the app is down until something recreates the Pod. You do not want that "something" to be you, woken up during an incident. You want the cluster to notice and replace it in seconds.
  • Scale. One Pod serves as much traffic as one Pod can. When load grows, you need several identical copies behind one address, sharing the work. You saw the frontend degrade gracefully in M1. Graceful or not, one instance has a fixed ceiling.

Both of these come down to the same thing: keep N identical copies of this Pod running at all times, and replace any that disappear. That one sentence is the entire job of a ReplicaSet.

The analogy: a lifeguard supervisor keeping the rota full​

Imagine a public pool that must always have three lifeguards on the deck, by rule. The person who enforces that is the shift supervisor. The supervisor does not personally watch the water. Their one job is to keep looking at the deck and counting. Are there three lifeguards in red uniform on duty right now? If one goes home sick, the count drops to two, and the supervisor calls someone in from the back at once to make it three again. If the town decides summer weekends need five, you tell the supervisor "make it five", and they bring two more onto the deck. Tell them "back to three", and they send two off.

Three things in that picture are the whole lesson:

  • The desired number (three) is what you declare. The supervisor's job is to make reality match it, forever. This is the reconciliation loop from M3, doing a headcount.
  • The red uniform is how the supervisor decides who counts. They are not counting specific people by name. They are counting anyone wearing the red lifeguard uniform. That uniform is a label.
  • "Count the red uniforms on deck" is a selector, that is, a rule that matches by label, not by identity.

Keep this last point in mind, because it is the part people get wrong. The supervisor manages whatever wears the uniform. Put the uniform on someone, and they fall under the supervisor's count, whether the supervisor "created" them or not. Take it off, and they vanish from the count, even if they are standing right there. A ReplicaSet works exactly this way, and both of those surprises are real.

Labels and selectors: the mechanism under everything​

A label is a key-value tag (a small name-and-value pair) you attach to an object's metadata. You saw them in M3, and you were told they would matter later. This is later.

metadata:
  labels:
    app: fernwood
    tier: frontend

A selector is a query over those labels. tier=frontend picks every Pod carrying that label, no matter what created it or what it is named. This idea, manage by label, not by name, is the design choice that makes Kubernetes fit together so well. A ReplicaSet uses a selector to know which Pods are "its". In Part C, a Service uses the same kind of selector to know which Pods to send traffic to. In M8, a NetworkPolicy uses one to know which Pods a firewall rule applies to. One idea, reused everywhere: objects find each other by matching labels. Learn it once here, and every later object clicks into place.

The flip side is that labels carry real weight, so a wrong label is a real bug. A Pod with a typo in its tier label quietly drops out of its ReplicaSet's count and its Service's traffic, with no error. You feel this on purpose in the lab.

ReplicationController: the ancestor, and why it is gone​

You will still see ReplicationController (RC) in older docs and manifests, so know what it is. The RC was the original "keep N Pods running" object. The ReplicaSet replaced it, and the difference is exactly one thing: the selector. An RC could only match labels by strict equality (tier = frontend). A ReplicaSet supports set-based selectors too, such as tier in (frontend, backend), environment notin (prod), or "has this label at all". That flexibility is why Deployments (M5) are built on ReplicaSets, not on RCs. You will almost never write an RC today. You write ReplicaSets, and usually you do not even write those directly, because a Deployment makes them for you (M5). You write one by hand here on purpose, so the thing a Deployment manages is not a black box when you meet it.

The ReplicaSet spec: three parts that must agree​

Every ReplicaSet has the same three-part shape inside its spec. Read them together as a set, because two of them have to match, or the object is rejected.

apiVersion: apps/v1        # not core v1 — ReplicaSet lives in the apps group
kind: ReplicaSet
metadata:
  name: frontend
  namespace: fernwood
spec:
  replicas: 3              # 1. DESIRED COUNT — how many Pods to keep alive
  selector:               # 2. SELECTOR — which Pods this ReplicaSet owns
    matchLabels:
      app: fernwood
      tier: frontend
  template:               # 3. TEMPLATE — the Pod to stamp out to reach the count
    metadata:
      labels:
        app: fernwood
        tier: frontend    # these labels MUST satisfy the selector above
    spec:
      containers:
        - name: frontend
          image: fernwood-frontend:m2
  • replicas is the desired count. The controller keeps exactly this many matching Pods. Change it, and the controller adds or removes Pods to match.
  • selector is the query that defines "the Pods I own". The controller counts Pods matching this and compares the count against replicas.
  • template is a Pod spec with no name. When the count is short, the controller creates a Pod from this template, and names it frontend-<random>. It is an ordinary Pod spec, the same apiVersion/kind/metadata/spec shape from M3, minus the name.

Here is the rule that catches everyone once: the template's labels must match the selector. If they do not, the ReplicaSet would create Pods it does not count as its own, then create more because its count is still short, forever. Kubernetes refuses to let you build that. The API server rejects a ReplicaSet whose selector does not match its template. When you see that error, this is why.

Self-healing and scaling, in one sentence each​

Everything the ReplicaSet does is that one loop, closing the gap between replicas and reality.

  • Self-healing is the gap opening because a Pod vanished. You deleted one, or its node died. The count drops below replicas, the controller sees it, and it stamps out a new Pod from the template. You do nothing.
  • Scaling is the gap opening because you changed replicas, up for more capacity, or down to release it. kubectl scale replicaset frontend --replicas=5 edits the desired count, and the controller creates the difference. Same loop, same mechanism, opposite direction.

In the lab you watch both happen live. You delete a running frontend Pod and see a replacement appear in the same second. And you scale from three to five and back to three, and watch the controller add and then remove Pods. Then, in Part C, you put a stable address in front of those Pods, so their comings and goings are invisible to whoever is calling them. That is the problem Services solve.
