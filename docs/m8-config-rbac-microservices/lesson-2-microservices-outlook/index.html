The Microservices Outlook

▶ M8 — The Microservices OutlookOpen fullscreen ↗

You have spent eight modules building one small application. It started in M1 as containers on a flat Docker network, and it ends here as a set of independently deployed, independently scaled, network-isolated services with a real front door and hardened access control. Along the way, nobody stopped to say the word that describes what you built. This lesson does. It names the architecture, it is honest about what that costs, and it maps every pattern back to the module where you already built it.

There is no lab here. This is the part where you step back from kubectl and see the shape of the whole thing.

The analogy: a department store versus a shopping district​

Imagine two ways to run a retail business.

The first is one big department store. Everything under one roof: homeware, candles, kitchenware, and the checkout, all in the same building, sharing the same walls, the same power, the same front door. Walking from one department to another is instant. You just cross the aisle. There is one building to heat, one alarm system, one lease. For a small or medium business this is wonderfully simple, and for a long time it is exactly the right choice.

But it has failure modes built into the building. A burst pipe in kitchenware floods the whole store and everything closes. To grow the candle department you rebuild the shared floor, and that disturbs every other department while you do it. And you cannot give the busy candle counter more floor space without taking it from homeware, because they share one floor. Everything is coupled because everything is under one roof.

The second is a shopping district: separate shops along a street, each in its own building. The candle shop, the homeware shop, the kitchenware shop, each with its own door, its own staff, its own opening hours. Now a fire in one shop does not close the others. The candle shop can grow, or rebuild, or restock overnight without touching its neighbours. A busy shop can take a bigger unit while the quiet one stays small. Each shop can be run by a different team who never have to agree on one shared rebuild.

The cost is just as real. Customers now walk between shops, across streets, and those streets have to exist and be looked after. You need signage so people find the right shop, traffic control at the main entrance to the district, and someone responsible for the roads themselves. A single building had none of that overhead. A district lives or dies on it.

The department store is a monolith. The shopping district is microservices. And the streets, the signage, and the traffic control are not a side detail. They are Kubernetes. Most of what you learned in this course was how to build and run the roads between the shops.

Monolith versus microservices, honestly​

A monolith is one deployable unit. All the code, every feature and every module, builds into one artifact, runs as one process (or identical copies of it), and ships as one release. This is not an insult. Monoliths are simpler to build, simpler to test end to end, simpler to deploy, and faster at internal calls because a call from one module to another is a function call, not a network round trip. For a single team and a moderate codebase, a monolith is very often the correct choice, and starting with one is sound advice.

Microservices split that one unit into many small services, each owning one job, each deployed and scaled on its own, each talking to the others over the network. What you gain is real:

  • Independent deployment. Ship the backend without redeploying the frontend. A change to one service is a small, contained release, not a whole-system event.
  • Independent scaling. Give the busy service more replicas without paying to scale the quiet ones. You scaled the Fernwood backend on CPU in M5 while the frontend sat still.
  • Independent failure. One service crashing does not take the others' processes down with it. Fernwood was built to degrade: the frontend still renders when the backend is unreachable.
  • Team autonomy and technology fit. Different teams own different services and can even use different languages. Fernwood is Node, Python, and Java behind one front door, and none of the tiers cares.

What it costs is just as real, and pretending otherwise is how migrations fail:

  • The network is now in the middle of everything. A function call that never failed becomes a network call that can time out, arrive twice, or vanish. Every call between services is a new failure mode.
  • Operational overhead multiplies. One service was one thing to deploy, monitor, and secure. Ten services are ten of each, which is exactly why you needed orchestration, service discovery, ingress, and policy. Kubernetes is not overhead you chose for fun. It is the tax that microservices charge you.
  • Debugging spans processes. A single request now crosses several services, and "why was this slow" becomes a question you cannot answer by reading one log file.
  • Data is harder. One shared database is easy to reason about. Data spread across services is not, and keeping it consistent across service boundaries is one of the genuinely hard problems in the field.

Here is the honest summary. Microservices trade internal simplicity for operational complexity, and you take that trade when the benefits (independent scaling, deployment, and team autonomy) are worth more to you than the simplicity you give up. That is a business call as much as a technical one.

Twelve-factor: the principles under the patterns​

Long before "microservices" was a common word, a set of principles called the twelve-factor app described how to build a service that behaves well when a platform like Kubernetes deploys, scales, and runs it. You did not read the list first and then build to it. But you followed several of its factors directly, which is the best way to understand them.

  • Factor III: config in the environment. This is exactly what you did in Lesson 1. The backend's database settings moved out of the image and the manifest into a ConfigMap and a Secret, injected as environment variables. The image is built once and configured per environment from outside. That factor is why the same fernwood-backend image runs against any database without a rebuild.
  • Factor VI: processes are stateless. The Fernwood frontend and backend hold no lasting state between requests. That is what let you run three frontend replicas behind one Service in M4 and scale the backend up and down in M5. Any replica can serve any request because none of them is special. Being stateless is what makes horizontal scaling possible at all.
  • Factor IV: backing services are attached resources. Postgres is not part of the backend. It is a resource the backend reaches over the network by name, through a Service (M4) and a StatefulSet (M6). Swap the database host in the ConfigMap and the backend attaches to a different one. The database is a plug-in dependency, not a built-in.
  • Factor V: strictly separate build, release, run. Your image is the build. The image plus its ConfigMap and Secret is the release. The running Pod is the run. Because config is separate, one build becomes many releases, and a rollback (M5) is just running a previous release again.
  • Factor IX: disposability. Pods start fast and can be killed at any moment. Readiness and liveness probes (M3) and rolling updates (M5) all assume a Pod is disposable and replaceable, which is what makes self-healing and zero-downtime rollout work.

The pattern here is that twelve-factor is not a checklist you bolt on at the end. It is the set of assumptions that let a platform run your service well. Building Fernwood on Kubernetes pushed you into following it whether you named it or not.

What you actually built: the patterns, mapped​

Here is the payoff. Every core microservices pattern has a name in the industry, and you already implemented each one in a specific module. This is the whole course, restated as architecture.

Service discovery is how one service finds another without hardcoded addresses. In a shopping district this is the signage. You built it in M4: cluster DNS plus Services mean the backend reaches the database as db-0.db.fernwood.svc.cluster.local and the frontend reaches the backend as backend, by name, with no IP address anywhere. Pods come and go, IPs change, and the names keep resolving. That is the pattern that makes independent services findable.

API gateway versus Ingress is the district's main gate, and a difference worth getting right. In M7 you put Envoy in front of the app and routed by hostname and path. That is Ingress, and its job is routing: get the request to the right Service. An API gateway is a broader idea. A true gateway usually does more than route. It authenticates callers, enforces rate limits, changes requests and responses, and sometimes joins several backend calls into one. Ingress is mainly the routing slice of that job. Envoy, the data plane you ran, is powerful enough to do full gateway work and is the engine inside many commercial API gateways. But what you set up in M7 was routing, not a full gateway. Knowing the difference keeps you honest: you built the front gate, and a gateway also frisks and directs everyone who comes through it.

Resilience is staying up through change and failure. Two modules built this. M5 gave you rolling updates (ship a new version with no downtime), rollbacks (undo a bad release in one command), and the HorizontalPodAutoscaler (add replicas under load, remove them after). M6 gave you durable state: the Postgres StatefulSet with a PersistentVolumeClaim, so the database's data outlives any Pod. Together they are the difference between a system that survives a deploy, a traffic spike, and a Pod restart, and one that crashes at the first disturbance.

And isolation and access control is this very module. NetworkPolicy walled the shops off from each other except on the paths that should exist. RBAC and Service Accounts decided who may touch what. ConfigMaps and Secrets kept configuration apart from code. A shopping district with no locks on the doors and no rules about who can enter the stockroom is not one you would run a real business in.

Common pitfalls migrating from a monolith​

If you take one warning from this lesson, take this: most microservices pain comes from splitting too early, too finely, or along the wrong lines. The failures repeat:

  • The distributed monolith. You split the code into services, but they are so tightly coupled that you can never deploy one without deploying the others at the same time. You now have all the network cost of microservices and none of the independence. This is the most common bad outcome, and it comes from splitting along technical layers instead of business capabilities.
  • Splitting before you understand the domain. Service boundaries are expensive to move once data and teams have formed around them. Drawing them before you know where the real seams are locks in the wrong lines. Many strong teams start with a monolith on purpose and split only once the seams are obvious.
  • Ignoring the network's new failure modes. Every call between services can fail, and a design that assumes calls always succeed will turn one service's outage into a whole-system one. Fernwood's graceful degradation, where the frontend still works when the backend is down, is the small version of the discipline this demands everywhere.
  • Underestimating the operational bill. Ten services need ten times the deployment, monitoring, logging, and security work of one. If you are not ready to run that platform, the split will bury you. This whole course was, in effect, the operational bill made real.

None of these is a reason to never use microservices. They are reasons to split on purpose, along business capabilities, when the benefits are real, and to keep each service as independently deployable as the move from department store to district promises.

Program wrap-up​

Look back at the whole arc. In M1 you started with distributed-systems basics and a flat Docker network. M2 packaged and ran the app with Docker and Compose on a single host. M3 moved it onto Kubernetes as Pods, and M4 organised and connected it with namespaces, ReplicaSets, and Services. M5 made releases safe with Deployments, rollbacks, and autoscaling. M6 gave it durable state with a StatefulSet and PersistentVolumes. M7 opened a real front door with Envoy-based Ingress. And M8 hardened it, with external configuration, RBAC, and NetworkPolicy, then named the whole thing.

What you built is one coherent system: Fernwood Goods, taken from three containers on one machine to a hardened, self-healing, independently scalable set of services on a real cluster. That is the same journey a production application makes. Every pattern you would meet on the job, you have now built at least once with your own hands.

Where you go next is not new syntax. It is depth in the operational skills this course kept touching. The natural next steps are observability (turning the logs, metrics, and probes you already used into a system that tells you why a request was slow), delivery automation (turning the manual kubectl apply into a pipeline), and, as a system grows past what plain Services and Ingress can express, a service mesh (pushing retries, timeouts, and mutual TLS down into the platform). Each of those builds directly on what you have here. You already have the foundation they stand on. You built it, module by module, on Fernwood.
