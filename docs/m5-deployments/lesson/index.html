Deployments — Rollouts, Rollbacks & Scaling

▶ M5 — Deployments — Rollouts, Rollbacks & ScalingOpen fullscreen ↗

In M4 you gave Fernwood Goods self-healing and load balancing. A frontend ReplicaSet keeps three Pods alive, a backend ReplicaSet keeps two, and Services send traffic to them. That is a stable app. But it is not yet a releasable one.

Think about what you do at work. The app is running and serving customers, and you need to ship a new version. With bare ReplicaSets your only tools are blunt. You edit the ReplicaSet's image, and it does nothing to the Pods already running. Or you delete the Pods and let new ones come up, with a gap where the app is down. There is no "bring the new version up slowly". No "stop if it looks broken". No "put the old version back". Every one of these is something you need the first time a release goes wrong during business hours. This module adds the object that gives you all of them: the Deployment.

Why a ReplicaSet is not enough to release with​

A ReplicaSet answers exactly one question. Are there N Pods matching this template right now? It has no idea of "the previous template" or "a template I am moving away from". Change its Pod template and it will not move across gently. A ReplicaSet only ever reconciles toward its single current spec, that is the one state it is told to keep. So it is the right tool for keeping copies alive, and the wrong tool for changing what those copies run.

To release safely you need three things a ReplicaSet does not have:

  • A controlled move. Go from version A to version B a few Pods at a time. Check each new Pod is healthy before you retire an old one, so the app never drops below the capacity it needs.
  • Memory of what came before. Keep the old version around, kept whole, so going back is instant and not a rebuild.
  • A record of what changed. A history you can read during an incident to answer "what did we ship, and when".

The analogy: a head chef swapping the menu mid-service​

Imagine a busy restaurant kitchen in the middle of dinner service. The head chef does not cook themselves. Their job is to decide which menu is live, and to make the line match it, without ever closing the kitchen while people are eating.

Each menu is a fixed set of recipes. Menu version 1, menu version 2, and so on. On the line, a row of cooks each work to the current menu's recipes, turning out the same plates. When the chef switches from menu 1 to menu 2 in the middle of service, they do not stop the line. They bring one cook onto the new menu, watch the first few plates come out right, and only then move the next cook over. One at a time, so there are always enough hands cooking to serve the room. Now say plate after plate comes back from menu 2 because something is wrong. The recipe cards for menu 1 are still sitting in the drawer. The chef calls menu 1 back, and in seconds the line is serving the known-good food again. Nothing had to be re-written.

Map this onto Kubernetes and you have the whole module:

  • The head chef is the Deployment. It owns "which version is live" and drives the change.
  • Each menu is a ReplicaSet. One per version, pinned to one Pod template.
  • The cooks are the Pods, stamped out by whichever ReplicaSet is currently scaled up.
  • Bringing cooks over one at a time is a rolling update.
  • The recipe-card drawer, that is the old menus kept whole, is the retained old ReplicaSets. This is what makes a rollback instant.
  • The dated stack of menus you can flip through is the rollout history.

Here is the important idea about the shape of this. It is one more layer of the same pattern you already know. In M4 the lifeguard supervisor, that is the ReplicaSet, kept N guards on deck. The Deployment sits one level above that. It manages ReplicaSets the same way a ReplicaSet manages Pods. Declare what you want. Something keeps it true. Now stack two of those on top of each other.

What a Deployment actually is​

A Deployment is a controller that manages ReplicaSets, and each ReplicaSet manages Pods. You write one object, the Deployment. It describes the Pod template you want and how the change should happen. The Deployment creates a ReplicaSet to run that template. When you change the template, say you give it a new image, the Deployment does not edit the existing ReplicaSet. It creates a new ReplicaSet for the new template, and shifts replicas from the old one to the new one, a controlled step at a time.

That old ReplicaSet, kept around but scaled to zero, is not clutter. It is exactly what makes rollback work, and we will use it directly. Notice one more thing. Each ReplicaSet the Deployment makes gets an extra label, pod-template-hash, added to its selector, that is the set of labels it uses to find its own Pods. That hash is how a Deployment keeps two versions' Pods from being mixed up with each other, even though they share the same app and tier labels. Each ReplicaSet only owns the Pods carrying its template hash.

Rolling update vs recreate: what happens to Pod counts​

A Deployment has a strategy, that is the rule for how a change happens. There are two types of strategy, and they change how the swap actually runs.

RollingUpdate is the default. It is the swap-the-menu-mid-service behaviour. New Pods come up and old Pods go down a few at a time, so the app keeps serving all the way through. Two knobs, that is two settings you can turn, control how:

  • maxUnavailable is how far below the desired count you will let ready Pods drop during the rollout. Set it to 0, and the Deployment must never have fewer than the full desired number of ready Pods at any moment.
  • maxSurge is how many extra Pods above the desired count you will allow for a short while. Set it to 1, and the Deployment may run one more than desired while it swaps.

Let's make this real with the frontend. Desired = 3, maxUnavailable: 0, maxSurge: 1:

  1. The Deployment creates one new-version Pod (now 3 old + 1 new = 4 Pods, the surge of 1 is used).
  2. It waits for that new Pod to pass its readiness probe, that is a small health check that says the Pod is ready to serve traffic. Until that passes, it does nothing else.
  3. Once the new Pod is ready, it retires one old Pod (back to 3 ready: 2 old + 1 new).
  4. Repeat until all three Pods are the new version.

At no moment were there fewer than three ready Pods. That is the zero-downtime promise, and it comes directly from maxUnavailable: 0. The readiness probe is doing the real work here. It is how the Deployment knows a new Pod is actually serving before it trusts it enough to kill an old one. So a new version that never becomes ready will never push out the old one. And that is exactly what saves you from a bad release, as you will see.

Recreate is the other strategy. Kill all the old Pods, then create the new ones. There is a gap where nothing is running. You would never use it for the frontend. You use it when two versions really cannot run at the same time. Maybe they fight over a single-writer file lock, that is a lock only one process can hold. Or a schema migration, that is a change to the database's shape, means old and new code cannot both talk to the database at once. Recreate takes downtime in exchange for never having mixed versions live. The point is to know it exists, and to know why its downtime is sometimes the right trade.

Rollout status and history are first-class​

Because the Deployment drives the change, it can tell you how the change is going. kubectl rollout status watches a release and waits until it either finishes or you stop waiting. This is the command you run right after shipping, or the one your CI runs. So an automated deploy fails loudly, instead of reporting success over a rollout that has quietly stalled. kubectl rollout history shows the numbered revisions the Deployment has been through. Each revision matches one of those retained ReplicaSets. This is not just decoration. It is the audit trail you read during an incident to see what shipped and when, and the list you pick from when you roll back.

Rollback is just scaling an old ReplicaSet back up​

Here is the part people often treat as magic. It is not. When a release goes bad, kubectl rollout undo does something very ordinary. It takes the previous ReplicaSet, the one still sitting there scaled to zero, and scales it back up, while it scales the bad one down. It is the same rolling machine, run in reverse. Because the old ReplicaSet's Pod template was never deleted, there is nothing to rebuild and nothing to re-pull. Rollback is as fast as a scale-up, because that is all it really is. The recipe cards were in the drawer the whole time.

This is why "keep the old ReplicaSet around" is a feature, not waste. A Deployment keeps a set number of old revisions that you can configure (revisionHistoryLimit, default 10). It does this so that going back one version, or several, is always possible and always instant.

Autoscaling: closing the manual-scale gap​

In M4 you scaled a ReplicaSet by hand with kubectl scale. That works. But it means someone has to be watching the load and typing the command, at the exact moment, day or night, that traffic climbs. That is the gap the HorizontalPodAutoscaler, or HPA, closes.

Back in the kitchen, the head chef also watches the dining room. As tables fill, they call in more cooks from the back. As it empties, they send cooks home. Nobody had to phone the chef to ask. The HPA does that same thing on its own, without you. It watches a metric, usually CPU as a percentage of what each Pod requested, and it edits the Deployment's replica count to keep that metric near a target you set. Go above the target and it adds Pods. Fall well below, and after a cool-down so it does not swing back and forth too fast, it removes them.

Two things make the HPA work, and both are easy to get wrong:

  • The Pods must declare a resource request (for CPU-based scaling, a CPU request). "50% used" means nothing without a number to divide by. 50% of what? No request, no percentage, no autoscaling.
  • The cluster must have a metrics source (the metrics-server) feeding kubectl top. If kubectl top pods does not return numbers, the HPA has nothing to read, and it will sit at <unknown>.

The HPA and the Deployment are a natural pair. The HPA decides how many. The Deployment decides which version and how to change over. Two controllers, each keeping one declared truth true, layered just like the chef who watches the room while also owning the menu.

Where this leaves you​

You now have the object that real releases are built on. In the lab you will turn Fernwood's two ReplicaSets into Deployments. You will ship a new frontend version with zero downtime and watch old and new Pods serve side by side. You will ship a broken version on purpose and watch the Deployment refuse to finish the rollout while the old version keeps serving. Then you roll it back. You will put a backend Deployment under a real HPA and watch it scale up under load, and back down after. Then you will deploy a stock Spring Boot service with the exact same pattern. That is your proof that none of this cares what language your app is written in.

See you in the lab.
