Deployments — Rollouts, Rollbacks & Scaling

▶ M5 — Deployments — Rollouts, Rollbacks & ScalingOpen fullscreen ↗

In M4 you gave Fernwood Goods self-healing and load-balancing: a frontend ReplicaSet keeps three Pods alive, a backend ReplicaSet keeps two, and Services steer traffic to them. That is a stable app. It is not yet a releasable one.

Think about what you actually do at work. The app is running and serving customers, and you need to ship a new version. With bare ReplicaSets your only tools are blunt: edit the ReplicaSet's image and it does nothing to the Pods already running, or delete the Pods and let new ones come up with a gap where the app is down. There is no "bring the new version up gradually", no "stop if it looks broken", no "put the old version back". Every one of those is something you need the first time a release goes wrong during business hours. This module adds the object that gives you all of them: the Deployment.

Why a ReplicaSet is not enough to release with​

A ReplicaSet answers exactly one question: are there N Pods matching this template right now? It has no concept of "the previous template" or "a template I am moving away from". Change its Pod template and it will not gently migrate — a ReplicaSet only ever reconciles toward its single current spec. That is the right job for keeping copies alive, and the wrong job for changing what those copies run.

Releasing safely needs three things a ReplicaSet does not have:

  • A controlled transition. Move from version A to version B a few Pods at a time, checking each new Pod is healthy before retiring an old one, so the app never drops below the capacity it needs.
  • Memory of what came before. Keep the old version around, intact, so going back is instant and not a rebuild.
  • A record of what changed. A history you can read during an incident to answer "what did we ship, and when".

The analogy: a head chef swapping the menu mid-service​

Picture a busy restaurant kitchen in the middle of dinner service. The head chef does not personally cook. Their job is to decide which menu is live and to make the line match it, without ever closing the kitchen while people are eating.

Each menu is a fixed set of recipes — version 1 of the menu, version 2, and so on. On the line, a row of cooks each work to the current menu's recipes, turning out identical plates. When the chef switches from menu 1 to menu 2 mid-service, they do not stop the line. They bring one cook onto the new menu, watch the first few plates come out right, and only then move the next cook over — one at a time, so there are always enough hands cooking to serve the room. If plate after plate comes back from menu 2 because something is wrong, the recipe cards for menu 1 are still sitting in the drawer. The chef calls menu 1 back and the line is serving the known-good food again in seconds — nothing had to be re-written.

Map that onto Kubernetes and you have the whole module:

  • The head chef is the Deployment. It owns "which version is live" and drives the transition.
  • Each menu is a ReplicaSet — one per version, pinned to one Pod template.
  • The cooks are the Pods, stamped out by whichever ReplicaSet is currently scaled up.
  • Bringing cooks over one at a time is a rolling update.
  • The recipe-card drawer — old menus kept intact — is the retained old ReplicaSets, which is what makes a rollback instant.
  • The dated stack of menus you can flip through is the rollout history.

The important structural idea: this is one more layer of the same pattern you already know. In M4 the lifeguard supervisor (the ReplicaSet) kept N guards on deck. The Deployment sits one level above that — it manages ReplicaSets the way a ReplicaSet manages Pods. Declare what you want; something keeps it true; now stack two of those on top of each other.

What a Deployment actually is​

A Deployment is a controller that manages ReplicaSets, and each ReplicaSet manages Pods. You write one object — the Deployment — describing the Pod template you want and how transitions should happen. The Deployment creates a ReplicaSet to run that template. When you change the template (a new image, say), the Deployment does not edit the existing ReplicaSet. It creates a new ReplicaSet for the new template and shifts replicas from the old one to the new one, a controlled step at a time.

That retained-but-scaled-to-zero old ReplicaSet is not clutter — it is the mechanism behind rollback, and we will use it directly. Notice too that each ReplicaSet the Deployment makes gets an extra label, pod-template-hash, added to its selector. That hash is how a Deployment keeps two versions' Pods from being confused for each other even though they share the app and tier labels: each ReplicaSet only owns the Pods carrying its template hash.

Rolling update vs recreate: what happens to Pod counts​

A Deployment has a strategy that decides how a transition happens. There are two.

RollingUpdate (the default) is the menu-swap-mid-service behaviour: new Pods come up and old Pods go down a few at a time, so the app keeps serving throughout. Two knobs control exactly how:

  • maxUnavailable — how far below the desired count you will let ready Pods drop during the rollout. Set it to 0 and the Deployment must never have fewer than the full desired number of ready Pods at any instant.
  • maxSurge — how many extra Pods above the desired count you will allow temporarily. Set it to 1 and the Deployment may run one more than desired while it swaps.

Make that concrete with the frontend, desired = 3, maxUnavailable: 0, maxSurge: 1:

  1. Deployment creates one new-version Pod (now 3 old + 1 new = 4 Pods; surge of 1 used).
  2. It waits for that new Pod to pass its readiness probe. Until then it does nothing else.
  3. Once the new Pod is ready, it retires one old Pod (back to 3 ready: 2 old + 1 new).
  4. Repeat until all three are the new version.

At no point were there fewer than three ready Pods — that is the zero-downtime guarantee, and it comes straight from maxUnavailable: 0. The readiness probe is load-bearing here: it is how the Deployment knows a new Pod is actually serving before it trusts it enough to kill an old one. A new version that never becomes ready therefore never displaces the old one — which is exactly what saves you from a bad release, as you will see.

Recreate is the other strategy: kill all the old Pods, then create the new ones. There is a gap where nothing is running. You would never use it for the frontend. You use it when two versions genuinely cannot run at the same time — they fight over a single-writer file lock, or a schema migration means old and new code cannot both talk to the database at once. Recreate accepts downtime in exchange for never having mixed versions live. Knowing it exists, and why its downtime is sometimes the correct trade, is the point.

Rollout status and history are first-class​

Because the Deployment drives the transition, it can tell you how the transition is going. kubectl rollout status watches a release and blocks until it either finishes or you stop waiting — this is the command you run (or your CI runs) right after shipping, so an automated deploy fails loudly instead of declaring success over a stalled rollout. kubectl rollout history shows the numbered revisions the Deployment has been through. Each revision corresponds to one of those retained ReplicaSets. This is not decoration: it is the audit trail you read during an incident to see what shipped and when, and the list you pick from when you roll back.

Rollback is just scaling an old ReplicaSet back up​

Here is the part that is often treated as magic and is not. When a release goes bad, kubectl rollout undo does something very ordinary: it takes the previous ReplicaSet — the one still sitting there scaled to zero — and scales it back up while scaling the bad one down. Same rolling mechanism, run in reverse. Because the old ReplicaSet's Pod template was never deleted, there is nothing to rebuild and nothing to re-pull; rollback is as fast as a scale-up because that is literally all it is. The recipe cards were in the drawer the whole time.

This is why "keep the old ReplicaSet around" is a feature, not waste. A Deployment retains a configurable number of old revisions (revisionHistoryLimit, default 10) precisely so that going back one — or several — versions is always available and always instant.

Autoscaling: closing the manual-scale gap​

In M4 you scaled a ReplicaSet by hand with kubectl scale. That works, but it means someone has to be watching load and typing the command — at the exact moment, day or night, that traffic climbs. That is the gap the HorizontalPodAutoscaler (HPA) closes.

Back in the kitchen: the head chef also watches the dining room. As tables fill, they call in more cooks from the back; as it empties, they send cooks home. Nobody had to phone the chef to ask. The HPA is that reflex, automated. It watches a metric — usually CPU as a percentage of what each Pod requested — and edits the Deployment's replica count to keep that metric near a target you set. Cross the target and it adds Pods; fall well below and, after a cool-down so it does not thrash, it removes them.

Two things make the HPA work, and both are easy to get wrong:

  • The Pods must declare a resource request (for CPU-based scaling, a CPU request). "50% utilization" is meaningless without a denominator — 50% of what? No request, no percentage, no autoscaling.
  • The cluster must have a metrics source (the metrics-server) feeding kubectl top. If kubectl top pods does not return numbers, the HPA has nothing to read and will sit at <unknown>.

The HPA and the Deployment are a natural pair: the HPA decides how many, the Deployment decides which version and how to transition — two controllers, each keeping one declared truth true, layered exactly like the chef watching the room while also owning the menu.

Where this leaves you​

You now have the object real releases are built on. In the lab you will convert Fernwood's two ReplicaSets into Deployments, ship a new frontend version with zero downtime and watch old and new Pods serve side by side, deliberately ship a broken version and watch the Deployment refuse to complete the rollout while the old version keeps serving, roll it back, and put a backend Deployment under a real HPA and watch it scale up under load and back down after. Then you will deploy a stock Spring Boot service with the exact same pattern — proof that none of this cares what language your app is written in.
